General construction policy

Construction is a portfolio-planning problem. A building must be valued by the change it produces in the complete playable system, including the structures and actions it enables, the assets it protects, the resources it consumes, and the time left for those effects to influence victory. Fixed build orders and structure ratios are admissibility heuristics, not the final objective.

Objective

Choose the first action of the feasible construction plan with the greatest expected probability of winning. Compare complete post-plan states, not isolated buildings. Include a wait action so saving gold, preserving a weapon reserve, or allowing an in-progress structure to finish can beat immediate spending.

System roles

  • A City is troop capacity and a potential commercial station. Its value depends on capacity pressure, survival, and whether a Factory can connect it to useful trade.
  • A Factory is a rail activator and train source. Its value depends on connected City/Port destinations, relationship-adjusted trade income, diminishing train throughput, survival, and remaining payback time.
  • A Port is a commercial station and the access point for maritime expansion and defense. Its value includes the Warships, trade routes, and overseas actions it makes possible.
  • A Defense Post changes the outcome and cost of land combat on a particular frontier. Its engine bonus does not stack on a tile, so additional Posts are useful only when they cover threatened front tiles outside existing coverage. A relevant threat means a live incoming land attack—not border contact, an exposed frontier, or a nearby rival's total army. A Post without that active attack has no generic ratio value.
  • A SAM is interception capacity over a portfolio of protected assets and predicted missile trajectories. Coverage, completion time, cooldown scheduling, and hostile launch capacity determine its value.
  • A Missile Silo is launch capacity in the Silo-to-weapon-to-target chain. Its value depends on weapon affordability, target value, hostile SAM penetration, salvo timing, and the economic and defensive spending it displaces.
  • A Warship is maritime interception and route control enabled by a Port. Its value depends on live transports, hostile naval capacity, trade exposure, and useful reachable water.

Decision model

  1. Build one authoritative state containing territory and victory progress, gold and reserves, troops and troop capacity, construction slots, structure levels and placement, rail components and trade relationships, land and maritime threats, missile routes and cooldowns, and legal build/upgrade descriptors.
  2. Apply survival constraints first. A plan that cannot survive a preventable imminent missile, land, or maritime loss is dominated by a feasible plan that can.
  3. Enumerate legal build, upgrade, and wait actions. Expand complementary chains only when an action changes a real dependency: Factory-to-trade-station, Port-to-Warship, Silo-to-weapon, or SAM-to-protected-assets.
  4. Advance every plan to the next material game event rather than an arbitrary fixed depth. A material event is a construction completion, threat impact, weapon readiness, trade payoff, loss of a required asset, or victory-threshold transition.
  5. Rank resulting states lexicographically by survival, immediate win feasibility, expected win probability, and only then resource efficiency. Execute only the first action and re-plan from fresh state on the next construction decision.

A SAM level is authorized only from economic surplus. Surplus means spendable gold can pay the authoritative upgrade price and the full cost of the next eligible City, Factory, or Port at the same time. This applies to routine parity, planned coverage, generic density upgrades, and trajectory-reactive upgrades. An incoming missile changes which SAM level is valuable; it does not make the economy's principal free. Saving toward an economic structure remains pressure before that structure becomes affordable.

Validation

Candidate policies must be compared on identical seeds across inland, coastal, island, team, free-for-all, high-threat, nuclear, naval, comeback, and closeout scenarios. The acceptance metric is paired whole-game outcome, with survival, territory, troop production, gold production, construction timing, and decision latency as diagnostic metrics. A candidate is not better merely because one local structure count or resource total rises.

Reference experiment (2026-07-21)

A paired four-match Europe/Normal/team comparison used the same seeds, 50 Nations, 80 bots, four player teams, five million starting gold, and a 30,000-tick limit. The ordinary Impossible policy won two matches; the current Legendary per-building objective-vector policy won none. Removing the late-game dominance exception for coastal Factory growth also produced two wins, but made one shared winning seed finish 550 ticks later. These small samples do not prove that the ordinary policy is globally optimal. They do falsify two tempting universal claims: that a more elaborate independent score is automatically better, and that permanently suppressing late coastal Factories is an improvement.

The current self-play checkpoint is not construction-policy evidence: its selected evaluation contains no legal or executed build/upgrade candidates. It may become the value model only after long-horizon training and runtime-adapter gates prove real construction actions across the scenario suite.

A later paired low-gold experiment rejected reserving the next economic build before the first Missile Silo: both variants won one of four matches, while the reserve delayed the shared win by 710 ticks. A Defense Post experiment also showed why local mechanics and whole-game outcome must be recorded separately. Unlimited threatened-front coverage preserved the same win rate but produced mixed survival changes; it was narrowed to the existing threat-derived Post cap. The accepted change only removes zero-marginal overlapping coverage and improves placement within that unchanged cap. Its four-match win rate remained one of four, so it is a mechanics correction rather than evidence of a globally stronger policy.
