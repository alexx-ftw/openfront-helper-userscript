General construction policy

Construction is a portfolio-planning problem. A building must be valued by the change it produces in the complete playable system, including the structures and actions it enables, the assets it protects, the resources it consumes, and the time left for those effects to influence victory. Fixed build orders and structure ratios are admissibility heuristics, not the final objective.

Objective

Choose the first action of the feasible construction plan with the greatest expected probability of winning. Compare complete post-plan states, not isolated buildings. Include a wait action so saving gold, preserving a weapon reserve, or allowing an in-progress structure to finish can beat immediate spending.

System roles

  • A City is troop capacity and a potential commercial station. Its value depends on capacity pressure, survival, and whether a Factory can connect it to useful trade.
  • A Factory is a rail activator and train source. Its value depends on connected City/Port destinations, relationship-adjusted trade income, diminishing train throughput, survival, and remaining payback time.
  • A Port is a commercial station and the access point for maritime expansion and defense. Its value includes the Warships, trade routes, and overseas actions it makes possible.
  • A Defense Post changes the outcome and cost of land combat on a particular frontier. Its engine bonus does not stack on a tile, so additional Posts are useful only when they cover threatened front tiles outside existing coverage. A relevant threat means a live incoming land attack—not border contact, an exposed frontier, or a nearby rival's total army. A Post without that active attack has no generic ratio value.
  • A SAM is interception capacity over a portfolio of protected assets and predicted missile trajectories. Coverage, completion time, cooldown scheduling, and hostile launch capacity determine its value.
  • A Missile Silo is launch capacity in the Silo-to-weapon-to-target chain. Its value depends on weapon affordability, target value, hostile SAM penetration, salvo timing, and the economic and defensive spending it displaces.
  • A Warship is maritime interception and route control enabled by a Port. Its value depends on live transports, hostile naval capacity, trade exposure, and useful reachable water.

Decision model

  1. Build one authoritative state containing territory and victory progress, gold and reserves, troops and troop capacity, construction slots, structure levels and placement, rail components and trade relationships, land and maritime threats, missile routes and cooldowns, and legal build/upgrade descriptors.
  2. Apply survival constraints first. A plan that cannot survive a preventable imminent missile, land, or maritime loss is dominated by a feasible plan that can.
  3. Enumerate legal build, upgrade, and wait actions. Expand complementary chains only when an action changes a real dependency: Factory-to-trade-station, Port-to-Warship, Silo-to-weapon, or SAM-to-protected-assets.
  4. Advance every plan to the next material game event rather than an arbitrary fixed depth. A material event is a construction completion, threat impact, weapon readiness, trade payoff, loss of a required asset, or victory-threshold transition.
  5. Rank resulting states lexicographically by survival, immediate win feasibility, expected win probability, and only then resource efficiency. Execute only the first action and re-plan from fresh state on the next construction decision.

A SAM level is authorized only from economic surplus. Surplus means spendable gold can pay the authoritative upgrade price and the full cost of the next eligible City, Factory, or Port at the same time. This applies to routine parity, planned coverage, generic density upgrades, and trajectory-reactive upgrades. An incoming missile changes which SAM level is valuable; it does not make the economy's principal free. Saving toward an economic structure remains pressure before that structure becomes affordable.

Outside an earlier survival constraint, the opening economic floor is two Cities before ordinary Port, Factory, SAM, or Silo demand. This is a bootstrap constraint, not a permanent City ratio: after the second City, the portfolio model resumes. In 40 paired whole-game matches across inland, mixed, FFA, island, naval, and nuclear scenarios, the two-City floor lost no wins or survivals, added one survival, and was outcome-identical in 32 pairs. The only other material changes were confined to mixed Europe; combined average territory deltas were positive in both mixed-map batches (+1,243 and +146 tiles).

Raising that floor to three Cities was rejected in a later 24-pair boundary experiment. Two independent gpt-5.6-luna low-reasoning audits inspected all 48 terminal logs and agreed that 22 pairs were exactly identical. Inland Alps was unchanged. In mixed Europe, the third-City candidate delayed the first Port in the only divergent seed, changed later SAM/Warship ordering, and converted a win into a severe loss; scenario wins fell from five to four and average territory fell by 3,107 tiles. One low-gold FFA seed accumulated more gold and 691 additional tiles but still did not win, so overall wins fell from eight to seven while survival was unchanged. Two Cities therefore remain the demonstrated bootstrap boundary; unconverted resources do not compensate for losing a closeout.

Factory density remains 0.75 × City levels (before the separate coastal qualification) after testing the lower 0.50 boundary. Twenty-eight paired games covered low-gold FFA, inland, mixed, naval, Baikal nuclear, Four Islands, and nuclear-rich Europe. The candidate and baseline tied exactly on the primary portfolio outcomes: 13 wins, 20 survivals, 10 closeouts, and no comeback recoveries. That is not evidence to replace a working universal threshold. The lower ratio also had material diagnostic regressions in naval seeds, including one loss of 8,976 tiles, 1.302B gold, and 0.2467 drawdown ratio, without any primary-outcome improvement. Its apparent upside was mixed: it commonly displaced a Factory with a City, and one nuclear-rich seed gained 26,776 tiles, but neither result converted an outcome. A same-bundle nuclear-rich A/A run had zero per-pair game deltas, so the comparison is deterministic rather than host-timing noise. The 0.50 boundary is rejected; the current 0.75 threshold remains the demonstrated choice, not a claim that any static City:Factory ratio is optimal outside its portfolio gates.

The corresponding lower Port boundary, 0.50 × City levels, was also rejected after 28 paired games over the same inland, mixed, FFA, naval, island, Baikal, and nuclear-rich coverage. It improved aggregate outcomes from 13 to 15 wins and from 17 to 19 survivals, but a universal policy must preserve the portfolio's survival tails, not merely its mean. In the Four Islands comeback gate it converted a win, survival, and closeout into a loss with 310,373 fewer tiles; a second island seed also lost the only observed comeback. The candidate's first divergences consistently replaced an early Port with a Factory or City, showing the expected Port-to-economy opportunity-cost trade rather than an independent local improvement. The gains in mixed, naval, and nuclear-rich seeds do not prove that this uncompensated island failure is safe. Port density therefore remains 0.75 until a policy can retain the comeback and island-closeout gates as well as its aggregate upside.

Coastal Factory suppression was tested separately from the global Factory threshold. Raising the non-dominant-coast multiplier from 0.33 to 0.50 leaves inland targets untouched but advances Factory demand in Port-capable nations. Across 28 paired games, it produced no primary-outcome gain and reduced nuclear-rich Europe by one win, one survival, and one closeout. One seed spent the additional Factory capacity while gaining 1.604M gold and 3,951 tiles, yet still converted the baseline win into a non-closeout loss; another lost survival. The increased multiplier therefore cannot be justified by a local rail or cash result. The 0.33 multiplier remains the demonstrated coastal opportunity-cost boundary until an alternative preserves every primary outcome.

Routine SAM density no longer uses a static difficulty-to-City quota. Setting those ratios to zero in a 28-pair comparison added one win, one survival, and two closeouts without losing any primary outcome: mixed Europe converted a loss into a closeout win, while nuclear-rich Europe converted a death into a surviving closeout. This does not mean SAM defense is optional. The candidate retained—and the terminal logs exercised—built-hostile-Silo, demonstrated-capacity, and active-missile paths: the improved nuclear-rich seed built three launchers and performed five SAM upgrades while hostile silo capacity and active missiles were observed. The policy therefore waits on speculative routine SAM spending, but keeps immediate threat-driven coverage and minimum capacity as non-negotiable portfolio actions.

Validation

Candidate policies must be compared on identical seeds across inland, coastal, island, team, free-for-all, high-threat, nuclear, naval, comeback, and closeout scenarios. The acceptance metric is paired whole-game outcome, with survival, territory, troop production, gold production, construction timing, and decision latency as diagnostic metrics. A candidate is not better merely because one local structure count or resource total rises.

Reference experiment (2026-07-21)

A paired four-match Europe/Normal/team comparison used the same seeds, 50 Nations, 80 bots, four player teams, five million starting gold, and a 30,000-tick limit. The ordinary Impossible policy won two matches; the current Legendary per-building objective-vector policy won none. Removing the late-game dominance exception for coastal Factory growth also produced two wins, but made one shared winning seed finish 550 ticks later. These small samples do not prove that the ordinary policy is globally optimal. They do falsify two tempting universal claims: that a more elaborate independent score is automatically better, and that permanently suppressing late coastal Factories is an improvement.

The current self-play checkpoint is not construction-policy evidence: its selected evaluation contains no legal or executed build/upgrade candidates. It may become the value model only after long-horizon training and runtime-adapter gates prove real construction actions across the scenario suite.

A later paired low-gold experiment rejected reserving the next economic build before the first Missile Silo: both variants won one of four matches, while the reserve delayed the shared win by 710 ticks. A Defense Post experiment also showed why local mechanics and whole-game outcome must be recorded separately. Unlimited threatened-front coverage preserved the same win rate but produced mixed survival changes; it was narrowed to the existing threat-derived Post cap. The accepted change only removes zero-marginal overlapping coverage and improves placement within that unchanged cap. Its four-match win rate remained one of four, so it is a mechanics correction rather than evidence of a globally stronger policy.

Trajectory and invasion experiments (2026-07-22)

Validation now classifies severe territorial drawdown, recovery, and sustained closeout from the real match trajectory. A paired eight-match Four Islands campaign contained one genuine comeback: the player fell from 54,261 to 21,291 tiles, recovered to 80% of the pre-collapse peak, and won. This seed is retained as a required portfolio gate rather than treating a scenario label as proof that a comeback actually occurred.

Two plausible universal invasion rules were rejected on paired whole-game outcomes. Blocking the first Missile Silo whenever a land attack was active lost one FFA survival and 15,615 average tiles, while an island pair changed from a win to a loss. Spacing successful Defense Post starts by their 50-tick construction duration preserved six of eight island wins but removed the only observed comeback and lost 4,209 average tiles. Offensive counterplay and parallel emergency construction therefore remain legal; neither local reduction in Silo nor Defense Post spending is a sufficient global objective.

The executable Defense Post contract now contains only the live-attack path. Obsolete border troop-ratio constants, topology scans, and preventive helper methods were removed after call graph verification proved them unreachable. A quiet border still returns no construction decision and performs no placement probe; a Post requires a live, non-retreating hostile land attack aimed at this player. Border contact or a nearby army cannot regain construction value through a dormant helper.

A paired twelve-match economic-upgrade experiment also rejected restoring the upstream density shortcut before placement exhaustion. The candidate executed 39 City, 9 Factory, and 26 Port upgrades. It kept the same four mixed-map wins, but reduced Four Islands from two wins to one and nuclear Europe from three wins to zero, losing 41,187 and 54,915 average tiles in those scenarios. Economic upgrades therefore remain a proven no-space fallback: a legal new productive site has priority over another level on an existing station.

The comparison harness now advances topology-search deadlines with deterministic work quanta. An identical-bundle A/A run remained bit-for-bit equal through tick 5,200 in nuclear Europe; host CPU timing is no longer allowed to choose a different construction trajectory. With that gate in place, a paired eight-match experiment tested removing the ordinary 50% delay before the first legal Warship while consuming the same random draw. Six mixed, island, and naval pairs were exactly unchanged because their baseline draw already admitted the same action. In the two nuclear pairs, unconditional construction preserved the single win but lost one survival and 32,056 average tiles, while displacing six Cities, three Factories, four Ports, and three upgrades. The random delay is therefore retained as a real wait action: a legal Warship without a live maritime emergency does not universally dominate continued portfolio growth.

A paired twelve-match SAM-economy experiment separated routine leveling from the one survival exception: a built hostile Silo whose demonstrated H-bomb capacity requires an already-built launcher to reach its minimum protective level. Requiring economic surplus and releasing the blocked slot bought 7 additional Cities, 11 Factories, and 15 Ports while avoiding 5 SAM upgrades in nuclear Europe. It preserved two wins, but lost one survival and removed the only comeback recovery in the four nuclear pairs; mixed and island outcomes were unchanged. The strict economic-surplus rule therefore remains universal for routine parity, generic density, planned coverage, and ordinary trajectory upgrades. The built-Silo minimum-capacity response remains a narrowly proven survival constraint, not permission to spend 3M on speculative SAM levels.
